LinkedIn Ads Review: Formats, Targeting and What It Costs

LinkedIn Ads puts four formats and job-title targeting in front of a billion professionals. On a first campaign, the setup order matters more than the creative.

The four formats, and the job each one does

LinkedIn groups its inventory into four named formats, and picking the wrong one is the most common first-campaign mistake. Sponsored Content runs in the feed and is the default for reach and engagement against a professional audience. Sponsored Messaging delivers into the message experience, positioned for engaging prospects where professional conversations already happen. Dynamic Ads are automatically personalised to the individual seeing them. Text Ads are the self-service option, described by LinkedIn as a way to create campaigns in minutes, which makes them the cheapest way to learn the interface before committing a real budget. There is also Boosting, a lighter path that amplifies content you have already published to new audiences rather than building an ad from scratch.

Why the Page has to exist before the campaign

LinkedIn's own getting-started sequence is two steps, and they are ordered deliberately. First build a company Page, which is where brand and trust live. Second, create the campaign inside Campaign Manager. People who skip the first step hit it as a blocker at the worst moment, halfway through building an ad. The Page is also what your ad points back to when someone clicks the advertiser name rather than the call to action, so an empty one leaks the interest you just paid for. Budget an afternoon for it: logo, banner, description, a handful of posts so the feed is not bare. Then sign in to Campaign Manager and the setup flow has somewhere to attach itself.

Targeting is what you are actually buying

The pitch LinkedIn leads with is scale, over one billion active professionals, but scale is not the differentiator. The differentiator is that this audience volunteers its employment data. Targeting by job title, function and industry is native here, where on other networks it is inferred from behaviour and sold to you as a lookalike. That changes how you should build campaigns: start narrow and by role, not broad and by interest, because a small, correctly-titled audience is the whole reason to pay a professional-network premium. LinkedIn positions its Lead Generation tooling on exactly that logic, that qualified leads come from a quality audience. The corollary is that sloppy targeting removes the only advantage you were paying for.

What LinkedIn does not put on the overview page

Pricing lives behind its own link and is not quoted in the advertising overview, so any specific cost-per-click figure you find on a third-party page is someone's account, not a rate card. Plan to read that page directly before committing a budget. LinkedIn does publish a substantial amount of supporting material worth using first: an ads guide covering specs for every format, a Success Hub of resources, structured learning courses with certifications, a dedicated ads blog, and separate entry points for small businesses and for agencies. There is also a partner directory of vetted integrations and services. None of that costs anything, and working through the ad specs guide before designing creative saves a rejected asset later.

Launching a first campaign in order

  1. Build the company Page
    Logo, banner, description and a few posts so the feed is not empty. This is step one in LinkedIn's own sequence and a hard prerequisite, not a nice-to-have.
  2. Read the ad specs guide
    Each format has its own dimensions and character limits, published in the ads guide. Checking them before design is cheaper than reworking creative after a rejection.
  3. Start with Text Ads
    LinkedIn describes them as self-service campaigns you can build in minutes. Use them to learn the auction and the reporting on a small budget before buying feed placements.
  4. Target by title, then widen
    Begin with the exact job titles and functions you sell to. Broad targeting on a professional network pays a premium for precision you then throw away.

FAQ

What ad formats does LinkedIn Ads support?

Four are named in LinkedIn's own advertising overview: Sponsored Content, which runs in the feed; Sponsored Messaging, which reaches people in the messaging experience; Dynamic Ads, which are automatically personalised to each viewer; and Text Ads, the self-service format. Boosting sits alongside them as a way to promote an existing post.

How much does LinkedIn Ads cost?

LinkedIn keeps pricing on a dedicated page and quotes no figures in the advertising overview itself, so this page will not invent one. Read that pricing page directly, and treat cost-per-click numbers you find on third-party blogs as one advertiser's experience in one market rather than a published rate.

Do I need a company Page to advertise?

Yes. LinkedIn's getting-started guidance lists creating a Page as the first step and creating a campaign in Campaign Manager as the second. Building the Page first also prevents the common failure where paid clicks reach a bare profile and the interest you bought goes nowhere.

How precise is the targeting?

Job title, function and industry are direct targeting attributes, alongside others, drawn from an audience LinkedIn describes as over one billion active professionals. That is the core reason to advertise here rather than somewhere cheaper, and the reason broad targeting wastes the premium you are paying.

Where do I actually create a campaign?

In Campaign Manager, LinkedIn's advertising platform, which you sign in to separately from your personal profile. LinkedIn also publishes a how-to-create-a-campaign walkthrough, an ad specs guide, a Success Hub and free courses with certifications, all of which are worth reading before your first spend.

Can I see what my competitors are advertising?

Not in Campaign Manager, which reports on your own campaigns only. Reachara is built for that gap: monitoring competitor ads and discovering creators and influencers near your audience. It does not buy media, so it complements the ad platform rather than replacing it.
